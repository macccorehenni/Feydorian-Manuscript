# Basic information

## Chapter content

<p>Time moves without regard for sorrow, indifferent to the weight of

regret. It does not wait for grief to settle or offer respite to those

who carry its burden. And yet, within the quiet moments—between breaths,

between choices—reflection finds its place.</p>

<p>Yae’s path was shaped by absence, by the losses she could not

prevent. Her mother’s sacrifice, her father’s fall—wounds inflicted by

fate before she could raise her hands in defiance. Destiny had pressed

upon her, steering her toward something greater, but she had never been

the one to shape it. That truth lingered in her silence, in the way she

measured every step forward.</p>

<p>Ferris knew grief too, though his was of a different kind. His sorrow

was not for a lost kingdom or fallen parents—it was for his brother, for

the one person tethering him to a past that no longer existed. He had

stayed behind, believing there would always be time. That Brell would

return. But the forest granted no guarantees, and the choices he had

made—the hesitation, the distance—had bound him in a cage of his own

making.</p>

<p>Their losses echoed each other, though neither spoke of it. In the

spaces between their thoughts, they recognized the same pain—reflections

of wounds not yet healed, grief shaped by quiet understanding.</p>

<p>Now, as they prepared to leave behind contemplation and step into

action, they did so not as strangers to loss, but as companions bound by

it.</p>

<p>Two figures once trapped in the past, now walking toward a future

waiting to be claimed.</p>

<h1 id="yae">Yae</h1>

<p>I regretted it all.</p>

<p>Ferris carried his grief like a weight just beneath his skin, and I

recognized it—because I had seen it in myself. That chair in his

tannery, the way his eyes lingered on it, the way he spoke its history

without needing words. Perhaps I was only projecting my own sorrow,

seeing my reflection in the wood, in the quiet space between our

words.</p>

<p>When my mother told me the truth of my birth, I felt—betrayed. It

took her pain, it took her dying breath for her to finally speak of my

true father, the man the world did not see. The king was never my

father. And in the year and a half since I fled Feydor, since I walked

away beneath a merchant’s shadow, that truth has carved itself into me

like an old wound that never quite closes.</p>

<p>I could have stood beside them. I should have. Not only for my

mother, but for my father, too.</p>

<p>Perhaps I am a demigoddess. Perhaps not. Either way, the past year

has been nothing but reflection, and now I see it for what it

was—indecision masquerading as patience. I was never just the hunted. I

became the hunter, seeking out my own suffering, circling the past as if

I could change it by staring it down long enough.</p>

<p>One piece of my mother’s story never left me. When Danche said to

her, **“You are destined for greater things, *but not by your

hand*.”**</p>

<p>He knew. Ten years before the lich rose, my father saw it.</p>

<p>And the more I allow myself to name him—**father**—the

more I understand that I have not lost everything. I see my grief—the

home burned behind me, the parents ripped from me, the identity

fractured but not wholly broken.</p>

<p>Now, I will face it.</p>

<p>And I will not be alone. Ferris knows what it is to be lost.</p>

<p>And if we are both lost, then perhaps, together, we will finally

learn how to move forward.</p>

<h1 id="ferris">Ferris</h1>

<p>Gods, I miss Brell. The more I think about him, the more I think

about Yae, and the grief we carry in ways neither of us fully

understand.</p>

<p>I’ve never lost a home, never lost a parent, but I lost my anchor. My

brother was everything to me. My family found time to be here for him,

slipping between their duties to the Forest. I could have joined them—I

should have joined them. There was no official training, no permission

needed. You protected the land simply because you lived upon it. And

yet, I stayed behind.</p>

<p>I used to think it was out of pragmatism, out of stability. But now I

know better.</p>

<p>The more I reflect on why I remained, the more I see something I

don’t want to acknowledge—__the Lich of Feydor in myself__.

I am nothing but selfish. I have delayed the inevitable in ways that

make me ashamed, and Yae saw it. Or at least, sensed it.</p>

<p>I feel like I am caught in a steel trap—razor-edged, unyielding. At

first, the pain is sharp, undeniable. Then it dulls into something

worse: certainty. You realize that pulling free will only rip you apart

further. That moving forward means tearing through yourself. So you stay

still. You tell yourself it’s necessary, that surviving is better than

bleeding.</p>

<p>But what good is surviving in a trap?</p>

<p>If I die, my mother and father will have no children, and I cannot

stomach the thought of them living through what Yae’s mother did—except

worse, because they would have never seen it coming.</p>

<p>I see the bars around me now, the cold, tightening grip of grief.

Staying is no longer a choice. I must move, no matter how much it hurts.

No matter what it costs.</p>

<p>If Brell were here, if he saw Yae—saw her resolve, saw mine—he’d be

proud.</p>

<p>I will not stay in the trap. Not anymore.</p>

<p>Thus, I joined her. Not as a craftsman, not as a man bound to the

past, but as something else entirely. A guardian. A warrior. A brother

avenging the fallen. I’m doing this for you, Brell.</p>
